Citations received in a calendar year to papers published in the two preceding years, divided by the number of citable items in those years (Clarivate / Journal Citation Reports). The most widely recognised journal metric in medicine and life sciences, but highly field-dependent — a "good" IF in surgery differs from a "good" IF in cell biology. Susceptible to manipulation via self-citations and editorial practices. The quartile shown in {{ config.get('app_name', 'this app') }} is determined by the SCImago CSV you uploaded, which uses SJR-based ranking, so the numeric value displayed here may not align exactly with the quartile tier.
Citations received in a 4-year window (current year + 3 prior years) divided by all documents published in those same years (Elsevier / Scopus). Covers a broader set of journals and document types than Impact Factor, including conference proceedings and book chapters. Because it uses a longer window and counts all document types in the denominator, CiteScore values are typically lower than the corresponding Impact Factor. Freely accessible via Scopus without a JCR subscription.
A prestige-weighted citation score developed by the SCImago Research Group, based on Scopus data. Inspired by Google PageRank: a citation from a high-SJR journal contributes more to the score than one from a low-SJR journal. Normalised for field and document type, making cross-disciplinary comparisons more meaningful than Impact Factor. This is the metric that determines the Q1–Q4 quartile in the Scopus/SCImago CSV, so it is the most internally consistent choice to display alongside the quartile badge.
A journal has h-index N if exactly N of its ever-published papers have each been cited at least N times. Unlike Impact Factor or SJR, it reflects the journal's entire citation history rather than a short recent window, making it resistant to fluctuations caused by a single highly-cited paper. Older and larger journals naturally accumulate higher h-indices regardless of recent quality, so it is best used as a measure of sustained long-term impact rather than current relevance.

How search profiles work

Each profile is a named PubMed query that runs on your fetch schedule. Papers returned by any of your profiles are added to your library, deduplicated by PubMed ID. You can have as many profiles as you like, and enable or disable them individually without deleting them.

Writing a good query

  • Use [MeSH] to match controlled vocabulary terms: "Artificial Intelligence"[MeSH]
  • Combine terms with AND, OR, and NOT: ("machine learning"[MeSH] OR "deep learning") AND "clinical microbiology"
  • Search specific fields with tags: [tiab] for title and abstract, [au] for author, [jour] for journal
  • Phrase search with quotes: "antimicrobial resistance"

Use PubMed Advanced Search to build and test queries interactively before pasting them here.

Changes take effect on the next fetch run.

How topic classification works

PubMed assigns controlled vocabulary terms called MeSH headings (Medical Subject Headings) to every indexed paper. The topic map below lets you translate those into your own meaningful category labels, which appear as coloured badges in your feed.

When a paper carries one or more MeSH terms that appear in your map, it receives the corresponding topic label. Multiple MeSH terms can map to the same label, and a paper can belong to more than one topic. Papers with no matching terms are shown as Unclassified.

To find the exact MeSH heading for a concept, use the NLM MeSH Browser. The term must match character-for-character, including capitalisation.

Topic labels are applied at display time, so changes take effect immediately without re-fetching.
